## 2. Story in prose

Long before the final experiment, humanity learned to make its waste disappear from sight. It never learned to stop producing so much of it. Discarded goods accumulated on Earth, obsolete machinery filled orbital routes, and climate damage deepened while each new cleanup project bought another delay.

Scientists engineered biological waste-eaters, provisionally called **Scavengers**. They were living creatures designed to break down selected waste and separate recoverable material. Ground facilities used them to process refuse. Orbital crews deployed them inside protected recovery pods and adapted station equipment to work through collected space debris. Their success became an excuse to keep discarding: cleanup capacity grew, but so did the waste it was expected to absorb.

In 2718, a separate scientific program developed an atmospheric treatment intended to help climate recovery. For five years, researchers refined it. The Scavengers were already widespread by then. Trials failed to reveal the full interaction between the treatment, residues in waste, and the organisms exposed to them. Society trusted another intervention to buy time while neglecting the harder work of reducing pollution and waste at their sources.

In 2723, the atmospheric release began. In this fictional setting, exposure caused DNA changes in the Scavengers and disrupted their normal feeding, growth and defensive behavior. They escaped their intended limits and attacked people, operating equipment and settlements. The change spread through exposed populations over the following years; it was not a single flash that gave every creature an identical mutation.

The altered creatures lost the limits that had once kept their waste intake selective. They seized nearby material without distinguishing refuse from useful equipment, digesting what their bodies could process and retaining much of the rest. A tough biological binding layer trapped plates, wires and other fragments around their living bodies. What they could not digest became external armor. Over subsequent generations, lineages whose inherited collection and binding traits produced protective shells survived and reproduced more successfully in the ruined environment. The metal never became alive: something living was moving inside it. Waste sites became feeding grounds and nests. Concentrated treatment residues at infested processing sites sustained exposure and made containment increasingly difficult.

Earth's atmosphere did not carry a cloud through the vacuum of space. Exposed organisms, contaminated cargo and residues on serviced hardware reached orbital cleanup installations through existing transport routes. The same waste-processing network that had been trusted to protect humanity helped spread the disaster. Orbital Scavengers sheltered living cores inside damaged recovery pods and scrap shells; their ability to function there remains part of the future technology of this setting.

By 2732, survivor estimates placed the loss of human life at roughly 99%. Attacks, ruined food and supply systems, exposure and collapsing shelter compounded the environmental crisis. Humanity had built systems to consume its garbage and treated them as permission to avoid changing its habits. The catastrophe grew from that dependence and the neglected risks of combining interventions.

Survivors reclaimed stations that could still be screened and defended. They linked habitation modules, repaired life support and established isolated approaches. Some orbital sites were easier to protect than settlements surrounded by infested surface waste, but space was never clean or universally safe. Their refuge became **H.O.P.E. Colony: Habitat for Orbital Preservation and Endurance**.

In 2733, you join its salvage fleet as a cadet. Recovered material keeps its ships, filters and shelters functioning. Your campaign leads toward the heavily contaminated nests and source sites that keep surrounding routes dangerous. Defeat their guardians, isolate the sources, and give cleanup teams a corridor they can hold.

The survivors cannot undo the neglect that brought them here. They can choose to stop repeating it. The campaign ends with a foothold for sustained cleanup and recovery, not another promise that a single invention will repair Earth overnight.

## 4. What the organisms can and cannot do

The owner selected a biological origin. The limits below are proposed world rules, not claims that such creatures can be made today. The earlier autonomous-machine origin is superseded; machinery now supplies shelter, tools and movement for a living organism.

1. **Life and DNA:** Scavengers are biological before the atmospheric release. The fictional treatment causes mutations in those organisms. Metal, plastic and satellites do not acquire DNA or become living tissue.
2. **Waste processing:** their original controlled behavior consumes selected digestible fractions and separates other materials. After exposure, intake becomes indiscriminate even though digestion remains limited; retained fragments are bound into an external shell. “Eats garbage” is the public shorthand, not a claim that every material is edible or that waste vanishes without byproducts. Metals can remain as shells and salvage.
3. **Needs:** active bodies require water, nutrients, energy and a suitable internal environment. Their ability to use recovery equipment and survive within protected cores is speculative. They do not reproduce or move forever without supplies.
4. **Hostility:** the fictional exposure disrupts feeding and defensive behavior. They attack humans approaching nests and damage inhabited equipment. Their aggression is not evidence that ordinary DNA changes inevitably create intelligent predators. They have no moral judgment about humanity.
5. **Space:** original orbital cleanup used protected pods/processing chambers. Mutated descendants retain a protected living core, stored resources and scavenged support hardware. Exterior shells may move in vacuum; living tissue does not casually breathe space. Lost protection leads toward inactivity or death in the fiction, without adding an oxygen meter to gameplay.
6. **Movement:** orbital enemies repurpose recovery thrusters or other onboard propulsion; ground organisms crawl or manipulate waste. They do not swim through empty space by pushing against nonexistent air. Existing shooter movement remains the gameplay abstraction.
7. **Spread:** treatment exposure starts in the atmosphere and on contaminated surfaces. Cargo and transported organisms carry it to orbit. Broad exposure affects the deployed populations over time; no magical simultaneous signal reaches every organism everywhere.
8. **Sources:** large infested waste sites retain concentrated treatment residues, feedstock and nests. Bosses are heavily altered organisms guarding or occupying these sites. Residue dispersal and local offspring explain renewed threats; no unlimited mass or chemical production appears from nothing.
9. **Containment:** combat stops immediate threats; isolating an infested source stops its local dispersal and scripted reinforcements. Existing enemies do not instantly die or revert to their original biology. Recovery crews still have waste, residues and infrastructure to manage.
10. **Salvage:** collect the recoverable nonliving fraction through sealed handling. Biological tissue is not a new currency, consumable, crafting material or corpse-harvesting requirement.

Use “Scavengers” for the original waste-eaters and “mutated Scavengers” for their hostile descendants. “Scrap creatures” remains survivor slang for their metal-covered silhouettes. No real organism, gene, mutagen, chemical formula or laboratory procedure is prescribed. Exact species design, body plan and final names remain content decisions.
### 4.1 Why the monsters look like scrap: intake, armor and evolution

**Confirmed direction:** chemical exposure alters the organisms; they begin taking in material indiscriminately and incorporate scrap as armor. **Proposed biological explanation:** their existing waste-gathering and material-binding behavior becomes excessive, while useful distinctions between digestible feedstock, rejected fragments and occupied equipment break down. This is a fictional organism, not a real engineering specification.

The causal sequence for writers and artists is:

1. **Before exposure:** a living waste-eater gathers refuse, digests suitable fractions and releases separated leftovers. Its original binding material helps hold collected fragments while processing them.
2. **Initial change:** the fictional treatment causes mutations and disrupted behavior. Some affected creatures continue collecting instead of stopping or shedding rejected material. Their intake becomes broad and uncontrolled; they do not possess a moral concept of ownership or distinguish a settlement from a source of material.
3. **Armor formation:** fibrous tissue and an adhesive biological matrix fasten retained fragments around the body. Metal plates overlap, cables reinforce the mass, and intact housings protect the core. The binding tissue is alive; the attached metal is not. “Absorbing everything” means gathering, engulfing and incorporating available material, not converting every substance into flesh or fuel.
4. **Survival advantage:** some shell arrangements protect the organism; others burden or injure it. More scrap is not automatically better. Shell weight, mobility, resources and the needs of the living core still limit growth.
5. **Evolution over generations:** during the years after 2723, inherited collection/binding traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common in some populations. The chemical initiates the fictional mutation crisis; selection acts across generations. An individual acquiring new armor is changing its body covering, not instantly evolving into a new species. Particular plates are gathered anew, not inherited as genetic metal.
6. **Present-day enemies:** the visible monster is a living organism inside an accumulated scrap shell. Local waste determines its silhouette: a canister, appliance housing or structural cluster can dominate the shape without becoming a separate living object. Large bosses can accumulate extensive shells around larger living bodies, subject to their later authored designs.

**Player-facing lore draft:** “The treatment changed more than their hunger. They stopped sorting and began taking everything within reach. What they could digest fed them. What they could not, they bound around themselves. Over generations, the survivors wore our waste as armor.”

This gives the accepted scrap-monster designs a biological explanation. It does not require new sprites, per-piece armor simulation, material absorption during combat, regeneration, a second health bar, armor damage reduction or additional loot. Existing reward amounts and hit behavior remain unchanged until a separate gameplay instruction authorizes changes.

The shell is not sufficient by itself to sustain life in vacuum: protected cores and retained recovery-pod support remain necessary in the orbital fiction. Its industrial appearance can largely hide the body; exposed tissue or seams are optional later storytelling details, not a demand to redraw every monster.

## 5. How HOPE operates

The player remains inside their ship. The safe hub is a protected orbital approach around a mothership, with inhabited station modules and Earth visible behind it. Docking services are places in that space, not an unexplained walking interior.

| Location or detail | Player-facing purpose | Story explanation | Scope boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central mothership berth | Spawn and Return Home arrival | Cadet operations and screened entry | One recognizable arrival landmark |
| Habitation ring/modules | Communicate a surviving community | Reused stations with people, food production and life support | Background animation, not colony management |
| Repair berth | Paid hull/shield services | Recovered material and technician labor are scarce | No free passive hull repair while parked |
| Skill workshop | Targeted ranks and rare recovered systems | Technicians repurpose usable components | One trade currency; no additional crafting tree |
| Route gates (“portals”) | Five mode selections and Resume route | Mission navigation/transfer beacons | Visual transition abstraction; do not promise real wormhole physics |
| Screening/processing detail | Explain safe salvage handling | Sealed cargo passes through controlled recovery equipment | Flavor only; no new contamination meter or handling fee |
| Survey craft | Establish long-term restoration effort | Scouts and cleanup crews use cleared routes | No NPC escort mechanics implied |

Arrival text: **“H.O.P.E. Colony — Cadet Operations.”** Repair flavor: **“Every repair keeps a pilot in the fight.”** Launch flavor: **“Recover what you can. Come home.”** Keep objectives, prices and blockers more prominent than flavor.

The safe hub suspends the expedition as specified in gameplay. Background service traffic does not attack, drop rewards or advance combat timers. Quarantine is the reason the safe hub can exist; it is not a new resource to grind. The shop and repairs represent resources available this run; there is no persistent account economy.

Earlier statements that the owner must supply every concept are superseded; final concept approval and implementation remain pending.

Keep the existing **provisional ten-wave campaign with boss encounters at waves five and ten**. Two source sectors fit that scope. The owner still supplies actual boss identities and attacks. “Source Guardian A/B” are planning placeholders, never final shipped labels. Do not restore the older boss names as approved content.

| Stage | Player objective | Source relationship | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Waves 1-4: approach sector | Clear the scheduled encounters | Defend the path to an orbital processing relay | Each wave clears only when required hostiles are resolved |
| Wave 5: first source sector | Defeat Guardian A and its required adds | An infested processing relay spreads exposed organisms and residue into the approach | Stop source emissions, finish remaining required hostiles, then mark sector contained |
| Waves 6-9: return corridor | Clear the deeper scheduled encounters | Reach the deployment/recovery infrastructure controlling a surface approach | No need to implement a separate ground-flight game |
| Wave 10: primary source sector | Defeat Guardian B and required adds | A major nest and residue reservoir sustain new threats along the corridor | Contain source, resolve encounter once, enter victory sequence |

**Biological source interpretation:** each source sector includes a nest and contaminated processing infrastructure. Guardian defeat triggers isolation of that sector's dispersal equipment and nest exits. This is encounter logic and presentation, not a new breeding simulation or a claim that one organism's death sterilizes the region.

**Proposed source-resolution contract:** defeating a guardian disables that sector's scripted source emitter immediately. It stops future source-created adds; it does not erase existing enemies, reduce every enemy's HP, reroll loot or clear unrelated pickups. Required surviving hostiles must still be defeated. Their destruction then permits the sector to finalize. Ordinary spawns already scheduled for later sectors retain their authored budgets.

The source's visual shutdown and a brief **“Source isolated”** objective message communicate progress. Neutralization is automatic on valid encounter completion; the player does not need a random key drop, a shop item, an inventory slot or an extra interaction minigame. A no-loot kill, including Space Collapse, can still resolve a source objective.

**Why later fighting is easier in the story:** fewer active sources means less local replenishment and a route cleanup teams can hold. This is not a promise that later campaign waves have lower difficulty. Later sectors can contain stronger preexisting enemies. Difficulty data still controls their combat numbers.

### Completion ordering for the later implementation

1. Register guardian death once through the shared encounter system; stop its future source emissions immediately.
2. Process rewards only if the weapon/death cause permits them. Story completion never depends on receiving loot.
3. Resolve all required surviving adds. Optional ordinary pickups are not hostiles and do not block completion.
4. When the final required hostile is gone, complete the sector once. A dead player cannot be rescued by a cutscene; the gameplay handoff proposes player death taking priority over victory in the same simulation step. Adopt that rule consistently; a simultaneous final kill cannot rescue a dead player.
5. At the midpoint, persist the contained-source flag through pause, hub return and same-expedition resume. At the final sector, stop further combat spawns and freeze the result before the ending.
6. Ending playback cannot award pickups, heal the player, advance scores or mutate the run. New Run clears sector flags; replaying presentation does not rerun rewards.

Boss-source flags are run-local. Swarm, Survival, Boss and Chaos do not inherit a campaign clear or modify a saved Earth state. Their fiction is repeatable patrol, route defense and local interception. All enabled modes remain accessible from the start.

## 7. Scrap as a story resource

Scrap has recognizable material families, described in [scrap types](../03-economy/scrap-types.md). Metal Fragments, Circuit Parts, Polymer Pieces and Power Cells connect recovered nonliving shell/equipment material to colony survival. Destroying enemies produces recoverable fractions, not proof that shooting is an efficient real-world recycling process.

The economy still has one trade wallet. Family labels explain recovered material; the four selected labels do not create separate currencies. Fixed salvage progress represents field adaptation and Return Home readiness. It remains separate from the random trade reward. Neither progress meter is a measured mass, electrical charge or scientifically calculated fuel quantity.
